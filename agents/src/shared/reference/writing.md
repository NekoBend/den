# Writing a document

Rules for writing a document that its reader can use,
in any language.
documenter reads this file at Step G3.

## Reader, goal, and scope

Before writing, decide three things,
and state them near the top of the document:

- the reader: their role, and how much they already know about the topic
- the goal: what the reader can do, decide, or understand after reading
- the scope: what the document covers, and what a reader might expect
  that it does not cover

Write for one kind of reader per document,
or per clearly separated section.
For readers outside the specialty,
use only words they already use,
and do not rely on them to fill gaps.

## Conclusion first, and a length budget

Put the conclusion, the recommendation, or the request
in the first paragraph.
Then give the purpose, the reasons, and the evidence.
Examples and fine detail come last,
or go into an appendix or a linked page.

State the main point directly,
even when the reader will not welcome it.
Do not open with background or with exceptions.

Decide the length before you write.
Keep only what most readers need,
and move secondary material out.

## Title and headings

The title names the specific subject
and the kind of document:
a report, a proposal, a request, a decision, an announcement.
Keep it to one line.

Each heading states the point of its section,
not only its topic.
Reading the title and the headings in order
must give the reader the content and the flow.

- Use at most three heading levels, and do not skip a level.
- Put body text under every heading.
- Write headings at the same level in the same grammatical form.
- Start the heading of a task with a verb.
- Do not end a heading with a period.

## Paragraphs and sentences

One paragraph holds one topic,
and its first sentence states that topic.
Three to five sentences is a usual paragraph;
more than about seven sentences is a signal to split it.

One sentence makes one point.
Put a condition before the main clause.
Prefer the active voice,
and name who does what.
Avoid double negatives.
A Japanese sentence that reaches 50 to 60 characters,
or an English sentence over about 25 words,
is a signal to check whether it can be split;
these numbers are prompts to look, not hard limits.

## Precision

Write numbers, dates, durations, and amounts as exact values
when the source gives them.
Write "and others" only after naming representative items.
Cut words that add nothing:
a phrase that says the same thing twice,
or a filler that carries no meaning.

## Terms

Use one term for one idea,
and keep it the same from start to end.
Define a term at its first use.
Write an abbreviation after the full name the first time,
for example "Continuous Integration (CI)",
and use the abbreviation after that.
For a technical term the reader may not know,
choose one of three:
replace it with a plain word,
add a short explanation at its first use,
or keep it because the reader uses it every day.

## Lists and tables

Use a numbered list when the order matters,
a bulleted list when it does not,
and a table when you compare three or more attributes.
Introduce every list or table with a sentence
that says what it holds.
Write the items of one list in the same grammatical form.
Keep a list to about two to seven items;
split a longer list into groups.
Give every table a header row,
and leave no cell empty:
write "none" or "not applicable" instead.

## Procedures

Put the prerequisites before the first step.
Write one action per step, as a command:
where to act, what to do, and what the reader sees afterward.
When a step can fail,
say what the reader sees and what to do next,
right after that step.
Say what the reader does not need to do
when they might think they must.

## Explaining a concept

Start with one plain sentence that says what the idea is.
Then show one concrete example,
and only after that state the general rule.
When you use a comparison,
say which parts match and where the comparison stops.
End with the exact statement the example was leading to.
Keep steps and commands out of an explanation;
link to a how-to instead.

## Writing in English

Use plain, common words,
and avoid idioms, metaphors, and humor,
which translate badly.
Keep "that", "who", and articles that a short form would drop.
Do not write "please", "simply", "just", or "easy"
in instructions.
Use sentence case for headings.
Use the ASCII hyphen-minus for dashes and ranges,
never the em dash or the en dash.

## Self-review

Before you send the document, read it again:

- Read only the title and the headings: do they give the content and the flow?
- Read the first paragraph: is the conclusion or the request there?
- Read as the reader: is any term used before it is defined?
- Read slowly once: does any sentence say something the source does not?
- Compare every number, date, name, owner, and due date with the source.
